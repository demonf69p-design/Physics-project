About

A thrust engine moves an object forward by throwing gas the other way. It powers aviation, space exploration, defence and transportation, built on Newton’s laws of motion: force changes momentum (Second Law), and every action has an equal and opposite reaction (Third Law).

Introduction

A thrust engine is a propulsion system that generates force to move an object forward by expelling gases or other matter in the opposite direction. It is one of the most important inventions in modern science and technology. When a rocket engine burns fuel, it produces high-temperature, high-pressure gases. These gases escape at very high speed through a nozzle in the backward direction. As a reaction, the rocket experiences a force in the forward direction, known as thrust.

Aim of the project

To understand the construction, working principle and scientific applications of a thrust engine. The project demonstrates Newton’s Third Law of Motion, explains how thrust is generated, shows energy conversion, studies fuel combustion and explores the importance of thrust engines in rockets, aircraft and spacecraft.

This equation comes from Newton’s Second Law: thrust is the momentum the exhaust carries away each second. Where F is thrust force, ṁ is mass flow rate, vₑ is exhaust velocity, Pₑ is exhaust pressure, Pₐ is atmospheric pressure and Aₑ is nozzle exit area.

Principles

The laws behind the engine

01

Newton’s Second Law

Force equals the rate of change of momentum, F = Δ(mv)/Δt. The engine throws out exhaust with momentum ṁvₑ every second, so it feels a thrust F = ṁvₑ + (Pₑ − Pₐ)Aₑ. On our test stand the mount balances this force, so the net force is zero and the engine stays in place.

02

Newton’s Third Law

Gas thrown backward pushes the engine forward with equal force. On our stand, the mount holds it in place.

03

Conservation of Momentum

Backward exhaust momentum equals forward rocket momentum.

04

Conservation of Energy

Chemical energy becomes heat, then the kinetic energy of exhaust.

Working

From fuel to flight

Step 1

Fuel supply

Fuel and oxidizer reach the combustion chamber.

Step 2

Combustion

Fuel burns into hot, high-pressure gas.

Step 3

Gas expansion

Gases expand toward the nozzle.

Step 4

Exhaust discharge

The nozzle speeds gases out backward.

Step 5

Thrust generation

An opposite force pushes the engine forward.

Step 6

Continuous propulsion

Thrust continues while exhaust momentum does.

Experiment

Balloon rocket

Thread a straw on a string, tape on an inflated balloon, and release.

🎈

Materials

  • One balloon
  • A long thread
  • A straw
  • Adhesive tape
  • Two supporting points

Procedure

  • Pass the thread through the straw
  • Tie the thread tightly between two supporting points
  • Inflate the balloon without tying its opening
  • Attach the balloon to the straw using tape
  • Release the balloon opening

Observation

Air escapes backward from the balloon, while the balloon moves forward along the thread.

Conclusion

This experiment demonstrates Newton’s Third Law of Motion and explains the basic principle behind rocket propulsion.

Pros & Cons

Power has a price

Advantages

  • High-speed transportation
  • Makes space exploration possible
  • Launches satellites
  • Powerful propulsion
  • Works in space with the right design

Challenges

  • High fuel use
  • Extreme heat needs special materials
  • Expensive launches
  • Hard to design and maintain
  • Noise and pollution

Future scope

  • Reusable rocket engines
  • Ion and electric propulsion

Scientists and engineers are developing more efficient, powerful and environmentally responsible propulsion systems, including reusable rocket engines, electric and ion propulsion, nuclear thermal propulsion research, more efficient jet engines, advanced heat-resistant materials and propulsion technologies for missions to Mars and beyond.

Conclusion

What we learned

The thrust engine is one of the most important applications of modern physics and engineering. It shows how Newton’s Third Law can be used to build technologies that move rockets, aircraft and spacecraft.

Through this project, we learned that thrust is generated by expelling gases at high velocity in one direction, producing an opposite force that moves the engine the other way. We also understood momentum conservation, energy conversion and gas dynamics in propulsion systems.

This project helped us understand the link between theoretical physics and real-world engineering. It also inspires us to explore the future of aerospace technology and scientific innovation.
